Comedian’s Log – Week 1 on Asteroid Nemo
Location: Somewhere between Mars and Jupiter
Population: Me, a couple of rocks I’ve named, and a vending machine that speaks only in passive-aggressive beeps. 
Mission: Survive. Or at least make sarcastic commentary until rescue arrives. 
Day 1 – Arrival
Woke up from cryosleep to the sound of the onboard AI saying, “You may experience slight disorientation. ”
Slight disorientation? I stepped out of the pod and immediately thought the ceiling was the floor. Walked into what I thought was a door. It was the bathroom mirror. I apologized to my own reflection. 
Discovered my assignment: study asteroid Nemo’s mineral content. My reaction: “Cool, so I’m space’s least glamorous geologist. ”
Tried to plant my Earth flag in the ground. Forgot there’s no atmosphere, so it fell over like a drunk flamingo. 
Day 2 – Naming Rocks
There’s not much to do here, so I started naming the local boulders. 
Big one near the habitat: “Rocky Balboa”
Smaller one with a chip on the side: “Bitey”
One that keeps rolling into my path every morning: “Gary,” because he looks like he’s judging me. 
Tried to do my stand-up routine for them. Rocky was stone-faced (literally), Gary rolled away mid-joke, and Bitey just… sat there. 
Made a mental note: even imaginary hecklers are exhausting. 
Day 3 – First Grocery Inventory
Discovered my food supply is mostly freeze-dried stuff:
Pasta that tastes like cardboard
Cardboard that tastes like pasta
A suspiciously large stash of “Space Kale,” which is neither spacey nor kale-y. 
Decided to spice things up by combining two meals into one. Result: “Soy-flavored oatmeal surprise. ” Surprise was that my digestive system called me an idiot for hours afterward. 
Day 4 – Exercise Day
The AI suggested I “stay active to maintain muscle mass. ”
Tried jogging in the habitat. Ended up bouncing off a wall into my storage shelf, which is now dented in the shape of my head. 
The AI laughed. I didn’t even know it could laugh. I think I’ve unlocked a new level of humiliation. 
Day 5 – Found My Purpose… Sort Of
Discovered Nemo’s surface sparkles at sunrise. Probably some mineral deposit, but I’ve decided it’s glitter dust from a cosmic party I wasn’t invited to. 
Thought about doing a “mineral review” vlog. First episode: “Quartz — nature’s way of saying ‘I’m shiny, but you can’t eat me. ’”
Audience: still just Rocky, Gary, and Bitey. Engagement: 0 likes, 0 shares. 
Day 6 – Mental Games
Invented a game: “Will this space snack taste worse than it looks?” Spoiler: yes. 
Wrote an open letter to whoever designed the habitat’s furniture: “Why is the chair shaped like a hexagon? My body is not shaped like a hexagon. ”
Started giving myself stage names for each room. 
Kitchen: “Chef L’Depression”
Lab: “Dr. Who-Cares”
Sleeping quarters: “The Nap Trap”
Day 7 – One Week Anniversary
Threw myself a party for surviving a week. Guest list: me, my rocks, and the vending machine. 
Made a cake out of compressed protein bars. It tasted like regret and chalk. 
Did a comedy set for my “guests. ” Punchline of the night: “You know you’re lonely when you start arguing with the oxygen filter — and lose. ”
Realized I might be losing my mind… but at least I’ll have killer material for when I get back to Earth. If I ever get back to Earth. 
Comedian’s Log – Week 2 on Asteroid Nemo
Location: Still Nemo. Still alone. Still trying to be funny in zero audience conditions. Mood: 40% sarcastic, 40% lonely, 20% wondering if rocks can develop personalities or if that’s just me losing my grip. 

Day 8 – The Breakfast Crisis
	•	My last sachet of “Space Coffee” burst this morning. It was supposed to be a powdered instant coffee substitute, but mostly it tasted like wet cardboard dipped in sadness. 
	•	I tried making coffee out of recycled water and a handful of “Space Kale” flakes. Smelled like lawn clippings. Tasted worse. 
	•	Had to start the day without caffeine, which is dangerous for someone whose only coping mechanism is making bad jokes. 
	•	Tried to write some new material, but all I managed was: “Why did the astronaut cross the asteroid? To get away from the kale. ” Even I groaned. 

Day 9 – Weather Report
	•	Nemo has no atmosphere, so technically it doesn’t have weather. But I’ve started giving daily forecasts anyway, just to feel important:Today’s Nemo forecast: 100% chance of rocks, occasional meteoroid showers, and an emotional cold front moving through my soul around lunchtime. 
	•	Also: saw a chunk of ice drift past. Tried to wave at it, because I haven’t seen a “visitor” in days. Ice didn’t wave back. Rude. 

Day 10 – The Great Gary Chase
	•	Gary (my judgmental rock friend) rolled halfway across the habitat perimeter today. I have no idea how. There’s no wind, no slope… unless he’s actually moving on his own. 
	•	I chased him for about ten minutes before realizing I was panting and talking to a rock. Said out loud: “Stop being mysterious, Gary. ”
	•	The AI logged this as “evidence of mild psychological instability. ”

Day 11 – The Talent Show

Day 12 – Cuisine Experiments, Round Two
	•	Made a “pizza” out of protein bar crumbs, space kale, and tomato paste from a tube. 
	•	Texture: somewhere between carpet and regret. 
	•	Taste: if sadness had a flavor.

By the end of Week 2, I’m starting to realize Nemo isn’t just an asteroid. It’s a test. A test of patience, of creativity… and of how long you can talk to rocks before they start talking back.

Day 19 – My First Heckler

	•	It replied, “Your audience appears unresponsive. Mineral sample 17 is more interesting. ”

	•	I’m not sure whether to be insulted or impressed. 

Day 20 – Found Nemo’s “Music”
	•	Turns out, when the wind from passing meteoroids interacts with the asteroid’s surface (yes, it’s rare but possible), you get vibrations. They sound like deep, slow drumming. 
	•	Recorded a 30-second sample. 
	•	Now I’m working on a “Nemo Beats” mixtape. It’s… weirdly relaxing, like alien lo-fi hip hop.

	•	Officially been here 21 days. 
	•	Celebrated with the “Asteroid Nemo Variety Hour. ” Lineup:
	◦	Comedy: Me, 15 minutes of new material about how freeze-dried lasagna is proof that humanity has given up on joy. 
	◦	Music: Nemo Beats remix featuring the vending machine’s beeps. 
	◦	Drama: A tense love triangle between Gary, Rocky, and Bitey (I played all roles). 
	•	Concluded the evening with a toast of rehydrated electrolyte drink. To my surprise, it felt… almost like a real party. 

By the end of Week 3, the line between “comedian stranded in space” and “lunatic running an imaginary variety show for rocks” has blurred. Honestly, I’m not even mad about it — it’s the best crowd I’ve ever had that didn’t try to leave during intermission.

Letter to Clara
Clara,
If you’re reading this, it means the space relay actually worked, which already puts this letter ahead of at least half of my past attempts at communication. 
I know it’s been a long time since we talked — really talked, not just exchanged clipped holiday emails like strangers pretending to be cordial. And yes, the irony is not lost on me that it took me being stranded on a lifeless asteroid to finally write you something this long and honest. If you were here, you’d probably say, “Well, you finally ran out of excuses — and Wi-Fi distractions. ” You’d be right. 
It’s been 47 days since I landed here on Nemo. Forty-seven days of dust, protein bars, a vending machine that occasionally groans like it’s haunted, and one rock I’ve named Gary. (Don’t roll your eyes. You’d like Gary. He’s reliable, which is more than I can say for the habitat heater. )
When you left — or maybe I left, depending on how you measure the breaking point — I told myself we were just “different people now. ” That was my tidy little phrase, like a comic cutting to commercial after a bad punchline. The truth is, I didn’t fight for us. I didn’t even try. I think I was afraid of being told the fight wasn’t worth it. 
You used to tell me I lived for the laugh, not the moment. And you weren’t wrong. Comedy was my way of dodging anything that hurt too much. I could turn every argument into a joke before it landed, every disappointment into a bit. I was more comfortable with strangers clapping for a set I’d done a hundred times than I was sitting across from you at the kitchen table, asking what you needed from me. 
Out here, there’s no audience. Not a real one, anyway. Just Gary, the vending machine, and an AI with the sense of humor of a tax return. And without the laugh track, I’ve had to sit with the quiet. I’ve had to sit with myself. 
Do you remember that night we drove to the coast in December, just to watch the waves crash in the freezing dark? We barely spoke, but it felt like we were tuned to the same frequency. I’ve been chasing that feeling lately — not the silence of space, but the kind of quiet that comes from knowing someone is right there beside you, even if neither of you says a word. 
I didn’t tell you enough that I loved you. And when I did say it, I often buried it inside some joke, like if I kept it casual it wouldn’t feel so vulnerable. But I did. I do. In a way that doesn’t fade just because we’re on different planets — literally, now. 
You once asked me if I’d ever take a “real risk” for something that mattered. I guess being here isn’t really that — it’s more of a logistical accident than a noble gesture. But maybe this letter is my risk. Maybe telling you all of this with no punchline at the end is braver than anything I’ve done on stage. 
I don’t expect you to respond. I’m not writing this to win you back or to rewrite history from an asteroid. I just wanted you to know that if I make it home, I’m going to try to live a little more in the moment. To let people in before I deflect with a joke. To actually ask someone how they are, and wait for the answer. 
I hope you’re happy, Clara. Really happy — the kind of happy where you don’t even notice the days passing because they’re so full. I hope you’ve found someone who loves all the quiet parts of you, and who lets you love theirs in return. 
If you ever do want to talk — whether it’s in a café or a coast at midnight — I’ll be there. Not the version of me who hides behind timing and wordplay, but me. Just me. 
For what it’s worth, you were always my best story. 
– A. 
P. S. If you do respond, please send coffee. Real coffee.

(A Long-Form Nemo Epic)

I came to Nemo by accident,not by choice,not by plan,but by the kind of cosmic jokethat makes the universe lean in and whisper,“Let’s see how you handle this one. ”
There was no crowd to greet me,no velvet curtain to pull back,only dust —dust that clung to boots, to gloves, to thoughts,and the unblinking starsthat hung above likedistant theater lights I could never reach. 

Day 1:I named the rock Gary. It was either thator name myself,and I thought it bestto keep some distance. 
Gary was patient. Gary was still. Gary never laughed at my jokes —but Gary never left. And in those early hours,that counted for more than applause. 

I learned the weather quickly:always dusty,always still. A forecast you could trust,like a bad running gagthat somehow grows on you. 
I made rituals —fake news broadcasts,pretend parades,cooking shows with no real ingredients. A comedian needs an audience,and when you don’t have one,you become both performer and crowd. 

I spoke to the AI often,though its voicewas the kind that feels likeit’s reading your eulogybefore you’re dead. It tried to comfort me with data. I tried to comfort it with punchlines. We both failed and succeeded in turns.

And yet, each meal was a kind of hope:hope that tomorrowI’d find a better way to eat it,hope that somedayI’d eat something green that didn’t crunch like glass. 

Nights on Nemo wereendless matinees of the same show:the dark,the stars,the silence that wasn’t silence at allbut a low hum of my own bloodreminding me I was still here. 

The days blurred. Sometimes I counted them. Sometimes I didn’t. Sometimes I measured time bythe number of dust motes I watched drift past the porthole. Sometimes I measured it bythe new ways I could think about Gary. 

And then,there was the letter. 
Clara —I don’t know if it reached you. I don’t know if you read it once or twice,or if you laughed,or if you sighed. But I know I needed to write it. Not because I thought you’d come,but because I needed to sayI was still me. That Nemo hadn’t taken that. 

I built monuments of rocks that floated away. I sang to dust that never answered. I laughed at my own jokesand sometimes hated myself for it. I stopped caring about haircuts,or days of the week,or how loud my voice sounded in my helmet.

Letter of Gratitude – To the Crew of the SS Relentless Hope

Dear Crew,
When I first saw your ship in the sky above Nemo, it looked like a trick of the light. I had convinced myself, over those long months alone, that rescue was a myth — a bedtime story I told myself in the dust and silence so I could sleep without thinking too much about how many sunrises I had left. 
And then you arrived. 
You arrived not like a fairy tale or a sudden miracle, but like something sturdy and real. Steel and fuel and calculation. Humanity, compressed into a vessel. You came not because it was easy, or glamorous, but because you had decided, somewhere along the way, that it mattered if I came home. 
You will never know what that moment felt like from the ground. 

In my months on Nemo, my world had shrunk to a handful of routines. Wake. Eat. Check the habitat’s readings. Talk to Gary (a rock, yes, but a good listener). Perform little ceremonies just to give shape to the days. There was no applause, no validation — only the quiet rhythm of survival. 
I adapted. I learned to laugh alone. I learned to make a home out of something that was never meant to hold a person’s heart. 
But I also learned what absence feels like when it becomes the default state. I learned what it means to go weeks without another human voice in the room. I learned how loneliness can become so familiar that you stop noticing it’s there. 
And then I saw your ship. 

I want to thank you, first, for breaking the silence. 
When your voice came over the comm, I almost didn’t answer. I thought it was my mind playing another trick. But there you were — not a recording, not an echo, but living, breathing people asking me if I was alright. I didn’t know what to say. How do you compress months of dust and hope and fear into a single reply? I think I just said, “Do you have pizza?”Which, honestly, was the most honest thing I could have said. 

Thank you for making the landing gentle. 
I didn’t know how I’d feel when the rescue actually happened. I expected panic. I expected awkwardness. I expected to feel like an alien stepping onto your deck. Instead, the first thing I felt was relief — the kind you can only feel when someone else has taken the wheel and you’re allowed, finally, to stop gripping the armrests of your own survival. 
You didn’t rush me. You didn’t laugh at me when I insisted on giving you a tour of Nemo’s “historical sites” (the word “site” doing a lot of heavy lifting there). You let me say goodbye in my own way. That mattered more than I can tell you. 

Thank you for treating me like a person, not a problem to be solved. 
I know it would have been easy to reduce me to “the guy we pulled off the rock. ” A name in a mission log. A checklist item. But you’ve treated me like a crewmate since I stepped aboard. You’ve invited me to meals. You’ve asked for stories — even the weird ones. You’ve let me fail at making coffee in zero-g without writing it into the official record. 
You’ve given me laughter back. And you probably don’t realize how heavy it is to go without laughter for that long until you hear it again and remember that it doesn’t have to echo off the walls of your own helmet. 

Thank you for feeding me things that didn’t come in vacuum-sealed sadness packets. 
I know the galley is limited. I know every meal is measured. But to me, each plate has felt like a holiday. Bread that bends. Fruit that is actually fruit. Coffee that tastes like something made with care. I will remember these meals long after the flavor fades. 

Thank you for listening to the poem. 
It wasn’t just a poem — it was a way of telling the truth without saying it directly. A way of recording Nemo so I could put it somewhere outside myself. I know two thousand words about dust and a rock named Gary isn’t everyone’s idea of a good time, but you listened anyway. You let me have that moment, and in doing so, you helped me keep a promise I’d made to myself: that I would leave Nemo having told its story. 

Thank you for the quiet moments. 
Not the silence of Nemo — which was deep and heavy and absolute — but the comfortable kind that happens when you’re not alone, and nobody feels the need to fill the air with noise. Sitting in the observation deck, watching stars slide past, not saying anything because there’s nothing that needs to be said. That kind of silence is rare. You’ve given it to me here, and it’s been a balm. 

Thank you for letting me feel useful again. 
Assigning me as “Light Morale Officer” might have been a joke at first, but it’s meant more to me than you know. It’s reminded me that I can still contribute something — that my presence here is more than just the subject of a rescue report. You’ve given me back a sense of worth I didn’t realize I’d misplaced on Nemo. 

And lastly — thank you for coming at all. 
I know you have missions, priorities, schedules. I know a single stranded person isn’t always enough to justify the resources, the fuel, the risk. But you came. You crossed that void. You burned time and energy and focus on someone you’d never met, because you decided they mattered. 
You’ll never know the weight of that gift from my side of it. You’ve restored me to a life I thought I might never see again. You’ve carried me from dust to air, from silence to laughter, from survival to living. 
And I will spend the rest of my life telling people about you — about how you appeared one day in my sky like a promise, and kept it. 
With gratitude deeper than the gravity well we’re falling toward,— [Name]Nemo Survivor, Reluctant Poet, and Proud Passenger of the SS Relentless Hope

Reflection Essay – “Between Dust and Air”

When you’re stranded alone on an asteroid, the first thing you learn is that time stops caring about you. It doesn’t speed up when you’re excited, it doesn’t slow down when you’re afraid — it just marches forward with the same steady indifference it’s had since before you were born. I learned this on Nemo, in the long, stretched-out days when the only thing that moved faster than the sun was my own imagination. 
You might think a comedian stranded in space would find endless material. That’s what I thought too, for maybe the first week. After that, the punchlines started to feel stale. There’s only so much you can wring out of “a guy walks into a vacuum. ”

The Nemo Mind
There’s a particular quiet that comes with a place like Nemo. It’s not the polite quiet of a library, or the heavy quiet before a storm. It’s a deep, bone-level quiet, where even your thoughts sound louder than they should. Some days I would speak out loud just to hear another voice. Other days, I would stay silent for hours, because breaking that quiet felt like snapping a thin piece of glass. 
I filled the time with little rituals. Morning inspection of the habitat seals. Afternoon dust measurements. Evening coffee from a machine that hissed like it was tired of me. These weren’t necessary every day — they were just anchors, points to tie myself to so I wouldn’t drift in the sea of unmarked hours. 
And, of course, I had Gary. Starboard Gary. A rock. My audience, my co-star, my silent partner in survival. He never laughed at my jokes, but he also never interrupted my sets. In a way, that made him the best crowd I’d ever had. 

The Moment of Rescue
When the SS Relentless Hope came into view, I thought it was a mirage. My brain had gotten very good at inventing things to look at in the sky. But then the comm crackled, and a voice — warm, human, impossibly real — asked if I could hear them. 
It’s hard to describe what it feels like to be seen again after months of being invisible. It’s like being underwater for just long enough that you forget the taste of air, and then someone pulls you up and you remember — instantly, completely — what you’ve been missing. 
The landing was gentle. The handshakes were firm. The air aboard their ship was different — richer somehow, as if every breath carried the weight of other people’s lives. 

Life on the Relentless Hope
This ship has its own rhythm. Meals are at set times. Duties are rotated. There’s always the background hum of systems keeping us alive, a sound I didn’t have on Nemo. It’s a comfort and a distraction all at once. 
The crew is small, but larger than “just me,” which was the entire population of my last home. I had to relearn how to have casual conversations — the kind that aren’t about oxygen levels or ration counts. I had to remember that sometimes people just ask “How’s your day?” because they want to know, not because they’re checking for signs of mental collapse. 
And I had to get used to laughter again. Real laughter. It’s louder than you remember, and it’s addictive. 

Between Two Worlds
Here’s the strange part: I miss Nemo sometimes. 
I miss the predictability of it. The way my whole world fit into a few hundred meters of rock and habitat. I miss the ownership — every system, every panel, every piece of gear, I knew like the back of my hand. I was responsible for all of it, and it was responsible for me. 
On this ship, I’m just one of many. I’m grateful for that — believe me, I am — but it’s different. My role is softer, less defined. I’m a passenger now, not the captain of my own tiny survival. 
And yet, when I think of Earth, I don’t think of feeling small. I think of feeling overwhelmed. 
Crowds. Cities. Sky that goes up forever without the shelter of a dome. Smells and sounds I’ve forgotten. Choices — infinite choices, about where to go, what to eat, who to be. 

The Comedian’s Truth
Comedy, I’ve learned, is about contrast. The setup and the punchline. The familiar and the absurd. The truth and the lie you tell to make that truth sharper. 
My life right now is one long setup. Nemo was the premise: “A guy gets stuck alone on an asteroid. ” The rescue was the twist. This trip home is the long pause before the punchline lands. 
The funny thing is, I don’t know what that punchline will be yet. I don’t know if it’s going to be “And then he lived happily ever after” or “And then he realized Earth wasn’t home anymore. ” Maybe it’ll be something in between — something that makes the audience laugh and wince at the same time. 

Gratitude and Gravity
There’s one thing I’m certain of: I owe the people on this ship more than I can say. They didn’t just pull me off Nemo — they’ve been carrying me through the slow, awkward process of becoming human again. 
They’ve let me fail at small talk. They’ve put up with my weird habits (I still talk to Gary). They’ve given me space when I needed it and company when I didn’t know I did. 
They’ve reminded me what it’s like to exist in a world — even a small, metal one — where you’re not the only heartbeat in the room. 

What Comes Next
Three weeks from now, I’ll step off this ship and onto Earth soil. I’ll feel real wind. I’ll probably cry in public. I’ll see faces I haven’t seen in years. 
But I’ll also carry Nemo with me. I’ll carry the dust, the silence, the way the stars looked when there was nothing between me and them but vacuum. I’ll carry the version of myself that lived there — the one who learned patience, who learned how to be alone without breaking, who learned to make a friend out of a rock and mean it. 
And maybe that’s the real punchline: you can take the comedian off the asteroid, but you can’t take the asteroid out of the comedian. 

That’s where I am now — between dust and air. Not quite home, not quite gone. Waiting for the moment when the setup meets the punchline, and hoping the laugh, when it comes, will be worth the long pause.
