The Porcelain Falcon
Miriam didn’t mean to buy the falcon. She went into the shop to get her watch repaired — though she couldn’t remember if it was broken — and left carrying a porcelain bird wrapped in brown paper. 
The shopkeeper had been certain she’d ordered it. He’d even shown her the receipt, with her name scrawled in a handwriting that was definitely hers except for all the letters being upside down. When she tried to explain that she didn’t need a falcon, he simply tapped the counter and said,
“You’ll understand when the tide comes in. ”
The tide never came in. The city was 200 miles from the nearest shore. 

Chapter 1 — The Man in the Red Chair
Miriam kept the falcon on the mantle, where it stared at the wall instead of the room. She thought about turning it around but didn’t, because the man in the red chair might notice. 
No one else believed in the man. He wasn’t always there, but when he was, he sat so still that Miriam couldn’t tell if he was breathing.  Sometimes she’d leave the room for a moment and come back to find the chair empty — but the indentation on the cushion deeper. 
The man never spoke, except for the time he said,
“Don’t wind the clock unless the window is shut. ”
She didn’t own a clock. Or a window. 

Chapter 2 — The Train That Didn’t Arrive
A letter arrived one morning, slid under her door without an envelope. It was just a single sentence:
“Your train leaves at noon, platform seven. ”
She checked her watch — still not broken — and saw that it was 11:45. There was no station in her city. Still, she found herself walking until she arrived at a row of benches facing nothing but a brick wall. 
A man in a station uniform was selling tickets for a train that, he admitted freely, had never once arrived. Miriam bought one anyway. 
When noon came, the brick wall shimmered, and for a moment she thought she heard the low, distant hum of an engine. Then the wall went back to being a wall. 

Chapter 3 — The Disappearing Cup of Tea
She returned home to find the falcon gone. In its place was a steaming cup of tea. She reached for it, and it vanished, leaving only the faint smell of lemon. 
A knock came at the door. It was the shopkeeper, holding the falcon. He swore he’d never seen it before. 
When she asked him why he’d taken it, he blinked as if she’d spoken in another language and said,
“You’ll miss the tide if you keep asking questions. ”
She reminded him there was no tide. He said, “That’s why it’s dangerous. ”

Chapter 4 — The Clock That Ran on Smoke
One morning the man in the red chair was gone, and in his place was a clock. Its hands were made of smoke, drifting slowly but never settling on a number. When she tried to touch them, her fingers went right through, and for hours afterward everything she touched smelled faintly of burnt sugar. 
The clock had no mechanism, no back panel, and yet she could hear it ticking — not steadily, but like a nervous heart. 
That evening, she heard it stop. The silence was heavy enough to bend the air. She turned around, and the falcon was facing the room now. 

Chapter 5 — The Ending That Happens in the Middle
One night she dreamed of the end of the story. She was standing on a shore (though she’d never left the city), the tide finally coming in, carrying dozens of porcelain falcons bobbing on the waves. 
The man in the red chair was there, but now the chair was blue. He told her it was time to wind the clock. When she said she still didn’t have one, he only smiled and pointed to the horizon. 
The sun was setting in the north. 
She woke up in Chapter 2 again, holding a ticket to a train that wouldn’t arrive, wondering if this was where the story had begun or where it ended.
